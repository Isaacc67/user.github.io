# Launch posts — driving traffic to the pre-order

Hours 6–40 of the Concept 3 validation plan. Three posts, one reply template.

**The rule that governs all of them:** lead with the insight, not the link. The post
must be worth reading by someone who never clicks. If you strip the link out and it's
still useful, it will work. If it isn't, it's an ad.

**Positioning.** You are not an agency veteran and these do not pretend you are. They're
written as a builder sharing a method and asking what's missing. Declare the commercial
interest up front — it costs nothing and buys you the right to be there.

---

## 1. LinkedIn (long-form)

Best channel: buyers are there under their real names, and long posts still get reach.

> Every AI-written draft sounds the same. People blame the model. It isn't the model.
>
> If you tell ChatGPT "write in our client's voice", you haven't given it information —
> you've gestured at information. It fills the gap with its own defaults. That's why the
> output reads like everyone else's output.
>
> The fix is writing the voice down in a form something can actually follow. Not
> "professional but approachable". Things like:
>
> • Sentence rhythm — average 14 words, never over 25, one idea each
> • Point of view — "we" is the company, "you" is the reader, active voice only
> • Evidence habit — every claim carries a number or a named customer
> • Banned words — revolutionise, seamless, game-changer, leverage-as-a-verb
> • House words — the terms of art this client actually uses
> • Structural tells — opens on the problem, never on the company
>
> That takes about 30 minutes to extract if you have a kickoff transcript and five pieces
> the client has already approved. You're reading their real patterns, not inventing a
> persona.
>
> The useful part isn't that AI follows it. It's that your freelancers can too — and you
> can check a draft against it instead of rewriting on instinct.
>
> I'm building this into a Notion system for agencies running content across several
> clients. Selling it as a pre-order first, because I'd rather find out now whether the
> problem is as common as I think it is. Link in the comments.
>
> What's on your banned-words list?

Put the link in the first comment, not the post. Then answer every reply.

## 2. Reddit — r/agency, r/freelance, r/marketing

**Read each subreddit's self-promotion rules first.** Several ban links outright. Where
they do, post this without the link and let people ask. A ban costs you the channel
permanently; a missing link costs you nothing.

**Title:** The reason freelance drafts always need a tone rewrite — and the fix isn't
"write better guidelines"

> Bias declared up front: I'm building a paid template around this. The whole method is
> below, though — you can build it yourself in an afternoon, and I'd rather it were
> useful than withheld.
>
> The problem isn't that freelancers write badly. It's that "the client's voice" usually
> lives in one person's head, and what gets written down is adjectives. "Professional but
> approachable" is not something a writer can execute, so four writers execute four
> different things and you rewrite all of them for tone.
>
> What actually works is extracting the measurable stuff from content the client has
> already approved:
>
> - Sentence rhythm: count them. Average length, ceiling, ideas per sentence.
> - Paragraph shape: how many sentences, where they break.
> - Pronouns: who is "we", who is "you", how much passive voice survives.
> - Evidence habit: what has to accompany a claim before this client accepts it.
> - Banned words: words conspicuously absent from approved work that competitors use constantly.
> - House words: terms of art appearing three or more times across samples.
> - Structural tells: how pieces reliably open and close.
>
> Roughly 30 minutes per client with a kickoff transcript and five approved pieces. The
> output is a page a writer can follow and an editor can check against — which is the bit
> that ends the rewriting, because now disagreements are about a rule instead of a
> feeling.
>
> The thing I keep hearing is "we have a brand guide, nobody reads it". Guides describe.
> This prescribes.
>
> For those of you running content across several clients — what do you actually hand a
> new writer on day one? I'm collecting how different teams handle this and the answers
> are all over the place.

The closing question matters more than the post. Replies are your buyer list.

## 4. Reply template — for people who describe the problem

This is the highest-value move available to you, and it isn't posting. When someone says
"my freelancers never match the tone" in a thread, reply properly:

> That's the exact failure — [echo their specific words back]. Genuinely curious: when a
> new writer joins, what do you actually hand them? I'm collecting how different teams do
> this and the range is wild — some hand over a 40-page brand bible, some hand over
> nothing and just edit hard for three months.

No link. No pitch. You are talking to someone who has just publicly announced they have
your problem. If the conversation goes anywhere, mention what you're building then — and
only then.

---

## What to track

Not upvotes. Track:

1. **Comments describing their own version of the problem** — these people are your buyer
   list and your interview pipeline
2. **Shares into private Slack groups** — the strongest signal available
3. **"Do you do this for [other problem]?"** — free product research, and possibly a
   better product than the one you're selling

## The honesty rules

- Never claim a number you haven't earned. No "I analysed 40 briefs" unless you read 40.
- Never imply you run an agency. You are building a tool and asking questions.
- Declare the commercial interest before anyone has to ask.
- If the pre-order fails, say so publicly. The people who watched you try are the
  audience for whatever you build next.
